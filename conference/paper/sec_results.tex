\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Design-space screening}
\label{sec:res-rank}

% results/analysis/v1/{mean_rank.csv,friedman.json} -> tab_ranking_v1.tex
Across the \FNScrVariants{} configurations of the screening study, evaluated
over \FNScrBlocks{} paired (dataset $\times$ seed) blocks,
\texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} attains the lowest mean macro-F1 rank
(\FNScrRank). A Friedman test rejects equal performance
($\chi^2=\FNScrChi$, $p=\FNScrP$), and the Nemenyi critical difference is
\FNScrCD.

That critical difference is why we describe this configuration as
\emph{top-ranked} rather than as uniquely best: \FNScrWithinCD{} of the
\FNScrVariants{} configurations lie within one critical difference of the
leader, so the screening identifies a leading group with
\texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} at its head. \texttt{Leaky/latency} in particular
is not separable from it ($p=\FNScrLeakyP$), and we treat the two as co-leaders on
terminal quality.

% results/analysis/v1/focused_tests.json -> tab_focused_v1.tex
The pre-specified comparison against the closest competitor,
\texttt{LeakyParallel/rate}, favours latency in \FNScrWinsA{} of
\FNScrBlocks{} screening blocks with a median difference of \FNScrMedDelta{} pp
macro-F1 (Holm-adjusted $p=\FNScrHolmP$). The hierarchical bootstrap over
datasets gives $[\FNScrBootLo, \FNScrBootHi]$ pp. The interval is wide because
the effect is strongly dataset-dependent. At the encoding level, aggregating
over all nine neurons, the block-level test favours latency ($p=\FNScrEncP$) while the
dataset-level bootstrap interval $[\FNScrEncCiLo, \FNScrEncCiHi]$ includes zero. We report
both: together they describe an effect consistent in direction but variable in
magnitude, which is a weaker and more accurate claim than uniform advantage.

One point of interpretation, because the opposite conclusion follows from the
wrong metric. On KDDCup99 rate marginally exceeds latency in \emph{accuracy}
($\FNKddAccRat$ vs $\FNKddAccLat$); on macro-F1, the pre-registered primary
metric for these heavily imbalanced datasets, latency leads by $\FNKddFOneGain$ pp. Fixing the primary
metric before the analysis is what makes that resolution credible rather than
convenient.

The screening therefore identifies a leading group rather than a unique leader,
and that is not an artifact of the Nemenyi resolution alone. Recomputing the
screening ranks over the \FNNoCtuBlocks{} dataset-seed blocks that exclude
CTU-13, the dataset the protocol audit shows to be most affected by the
screening partition, exchanges the first two positions: \texttt{Leaky/latency}
attains \FNNoCtuLeadRank{} and \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency}
\FNNoCtuChampRank. The margin, \FNNoCtuMargin{} of a rank, is consistent with
the pre-specified paired comparison that does not separate them
($p=\FNScrLeakyP$). We select \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} as the confirmation
target from this co-leading pair and do not claim our evidence resolves the
choice between the two. The encoding conclusion is unaffected: latency-encoded
configurations occupy all \FNNoCtuLatTop{} leading positions under both
aggregations.

\subsection{Strict two-axis confirmation}
\label{sec:res-strict}

Each axis holds one factor fixed, so neither, nor their combination, reconstructs a strict ranking of all \FNScrVariants{} configurations. That sweep
was not run, and the complete ranking remains the screening result of
Section~\ref{sec:res-rank}.

\paragraph{Encoding axis, neuron fixed.}
% results/analysis/v2_encoding_axis/
Across \FNEncBlocks{} paired (protocol $\times$ seed) blocks of the strict
encoding-axis confirmation, latency attains the lowest mean rank
(\FNEncRank{} of \FNEncLevels{} encodings, Friedman $p=\FNEncP$) and wins
\FNEncWins{} of those blocks. It records the highest terminal macro-F1 on four
of the five protocols. The advantage is not universal: on CTU-13 fold~1, delta
coding substantially outperforms both alternatives ($\FNCtuBEncDel$ against
$\FNCtuBEncRat$ for rate and $\FNCtuBEncLat$ for latency).

That inversion is part of the result rather than an inconvenient outlier: it
shows that \emph{encoding preference can invert under scenario-level
distribution shift}. We report it without proposing a mechanism. The data
establish that the preference inverts, not why, and no experiment in this study
would support a post-hoc explanation.

\paragraph{Neuron axis, encoding fixed.}
% results/analysis/v2_neuron_axis/
Across \FNNeuBlocks{} blocks of the strict neuron-axis confirmation,
\texttt{LeakyParallel} attains the lowest aggregate mean rank
(\FNNeuRank{} of \FNNeuLevels{} families, $p=\FNNeuP$). The advantage is narrow
and does not translate into per-block dominance: \FNNeuRunnerUp{} follows at
\FNNeuRunnerUpRank, \FNNeuMostWins{} wins more individual blocks than
\texttt{LeakyParallel} does, and on CTU-13 fold~1 the recurrent families are
markedly stronger ($\FNCtuBNeuRSyn$ for \texttt{RSynaptic} and
$\FNCtuBNeuRLky$ for \texttt{RLeaky}, against $\FNCtuBNeuLP$).

\paragraph{CTU-13 under the strict protocol.}
% results/v1_submitted/ctu13_strict_vanilla.csv
The largest effect of the strict protocol is on CTU-13. Under the submitted
protocol, whole-scenario host aggregates with a random flow split, \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} attains \FNCtuLegacy{} macro-F1. Making the
aggregation causal alone leaves \FNCtuCausalRandom. Adding a scenario-disjoint
partition brings it to $\mathbf{\FNCtuStrict\%}$, with a standard deviation of
$\FNCtuSdEval$ percentage points over the $\FNCtuNEval$ fold-seed evaluations.
Fold means, in fold order, are $\FNCtuIdxFA$, $\FNCtuIdxFB$, $\FNCtuIdxFC$ and
$\FNCtuIdxFD$. We report the latter with its fold range as
the CTU-13 result and retain the former only as a labelled protocol comparison.
The fold spread reflects genuine differences in scenario difficulty that a
uniform perfect score concealed, which is why we never quote the mean alone.

This four-fold figure is the strict CTU-13 \emph{performance estimate}. It is
not the same quantity as the CTU-13 rows of the neuron-axis table, which come
from two pre-registered folds and exist to rank neuron families against one
another; those are a comparative confirmation, not a CTU-13 performance number.

\subsection{Temporal evidence accumulation and spike efficiency}
\label{sec:res-readout}

This subsection separates two findings that are easy to conflate: \emph{when}
each encoding resolves its decision, and \emph{how much event activity} that
costs. The first is protocol-dependent; the second is not.

\paragraph{Temporal convergence is dataset-dependent.}
% results/analysis/early_readout/summary.csv
On strict NSL-KDD evaluation, latency reaches $99\%$ of its terminal macro-F1 at
a median of $T_{99}=\FNTNNNslLat$ timesteps against $\FNTNNNslRat$ for rate, a substantial opportunity for truncated inference, and the strongest such case
we observe. It does not hold uniformly. Table~\ref{tab:readout} reports all five
strict protocols: latency also resolves earlier on CTU-13 fold~0, while rate
reaches its terminal operating point earlier on KDDCup99, CIC-IDS2017 and CTU-13
fold~1. The timing benefit of latency coding therefore depends on the underlying
traffic distribution and is reported as a dataset-specific result.

On NSL-KDD latency's $T_{99}$ ranges from \FNTNNNslLatMin{} to
\FNTNNNslLatMax{} across five seeds, so the timing advantage is large in the
median but not uniform across initialisations; Figure~\ref{fig:readout} plots
every seed individually rather than a mean band, since the quantity is discrete
and skewed.

A short $T_{99}$ measures rapid attainment of a configuration's \emph{own}
terminal performance and does not imply high-quality early inference. Delta
reaches $T_{99}=1$ on NSL-KDD with a terminal score $\FNDeltaDeficitNsl$ pp
below latency's: rapid stabilisation at a weaker solution, not an
early-inference advantage. That behaviour is a property of the representation
rather than an empirical tendency. Applied to a static per-flow vector, delta
modulation reduces exactly to one-step amplitude thresholding: all input
activity occurs at $t=1$ ($\FNDeltaSpkNsl$ spikes per sample on NSL-KDD, against
$\FNNslLatInSpk$ for latency; the difference is the gap between the two gates,
$\theta_\delta>\theta_\mathrm{lat}$, not a property of either code, and
$\FNDeltaSpkAfter$ at every subsequent timestep across all
$\FNNslTestN$ test samples), and the emitted code agrees with the indicator
$x_i>\theta_\delta$ on $\FNDeltaPairs$ of $\FNDeltaPairs$ feature-sample pairs.
Its aggregate behaviour here should therefore be read as a result about one-step
amplitude thresholding on static tabular input, not about delta modulation on
temporally structured signals.

\paragraph{Synaptic-operation demand is consistently lower.}
% results/analysis/efficiency/summary.csv
Unlike timing, the efficiency result holds on every strict protocol, and three
quantities must be distinguished because only the first is structural.

\emph{Input activity} separates for a reason with a closed form. Between
$\FNZeroPctMin\%$ and $\FNZeroPctMax\%$ of features are exactly zero after
preprocessing, a consequence of one-hot expansion and of min-max features
sitting at their minimum rather than of any coding choice. Neither code pays for
them: $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0)$ is silent and a gated latency encoder does not
fire. The separation is therefore determined entirely by how the two codes
represent the \emph{active} features. Rate emits $T\,\mathbb{E}[x]$ spikes per
active feature and latency emits exactly one, so the ratio \emph{is}
$T\sum_{x>0}x\,/\,|\{x>\theta_\mathrm{lat}\}|$, independent of dimensionality
and of sparsity. This is an identity rather than a fitted model: the expression
is derived from the two encoders' definitions, and measurement is used to verify
it, not to estimate it. Measured across the five confirmation protocols, which span
$\FNDimCtuA$ to $\FNDimNsl$ dimensions and $\FNZeroPctMin\%$ to
$\FNZeroPctMax\%$ zero-sparsity, the ratio runs from $\FNInpRatioMin\times$ to
$\FNInpRatioMax\times$ and reproduces the identity on every protocol at a
residual consistent with floating-point and with the Bernoulli sampling noise in
rate's draw: the
absolute error is $\FNPredErrNsl\%$ on NSL-KDD, $\FNPredErrKdd\%$ on KDDCup99,
$\FNPredErrCic\%$ on CIC-IDS2017, and $\FNPredErrCtuA\%$ and $\FNPredErrCtuB\%$
on the two CTU-13 folds, for a maximum of $\FNPredErrMax\%$. The denominator
matters and is written explicitly: the numerator counts what rate emits over all
features with $x>0$, the denominator what latency emits over
$\{x>\theta_\mathrm{lat}\}$. These coincide wherever the gate band is empty, and
separate on CTU-13, where it is not.
The prediction also accounts for the range: CIC-IDS2017 sits at the low end
because its active features carry the lowest mean magnitude ($\FNXbarActCic$
against $\FNXbarActKdd$ on KDDCup99).

\emph{Whole-network spike count} is not structurally guaranteed, and the measured
gap is far smaller: rate emits only $\FNTotRatioMin\times$ to
$\FNTotRatioMax\times$ more total spikes. Under latency coding the hidden layer
accounts for $\FNHidShareLatMin$--$\FNHidShareLatMax\%$ of all spikes, and the
two encodings differ there by a factor of only $\FNHidRatioMin$ to
$\FNHidRatioMax$ (on CIC-IDS2017 latency in fact emits slightly \emph{more}
hidden spikes than rate), so the input-layer saving is largely diluted in the
total.

\emph{Synaptic operations} weight each spike by the fanout it drives, and this is
where the input saving reappears: latency's reduction occurs \emph{before} the
high-fanout input-to-hidden projection, where each spike drives $128$ synapses
against the readout's, which is $5$ on the five-class datasets and $2$ on binary
CTU-13. Input activity accounts for
$\FNInShareSopLatMin$--$\FNInShareSopLatMax\%$ of latency's operations but
$\FNInShareSopRatMin$--$\FNInShareSopRatMax\%$ of rate's. Rate therefore requires
$\FNSopRatioMin\times$ to $\FNSopRatioMax\times$ more synaptic operations per
sample than latency across all five strict protocols. The reduction in
operations is an order of magnitude larger than the reduction in raw spike
count: \emph{where} spikes occur matters more than how many there are. We report
this as an operation-count measure of compute demand; any conversion to energy
is an operation-based proxy (Section~\ref{sec:eff}).

Table~\ref{tab:strict-results} reports the full per-protocol metric detail for
both confirmation axes, macro-F1, MCC, detection rate, false-alarm rate and
synaptic operations, each as a seed mean with its across-seed standard
deviation. It is worth reading beside the mean ranks: on CTU-13 fold~1 both
latency and rate reach detection rates of at least $\FNCtuBDrMin$ at
false-alarm rates of $\FNCtuBFarLat$ and $\FNCtuBFarRat$ respectively, so their
macro-F1 there reflects near-indiscriminate flagging rather
than discrimination, which is the behaviour delta avoids on that fold.

\subsection{What spike timing is worth, in isolation}
\label{sec:res-timing}

% results/analysis/e1_timing_isolation.csv  (post-hoc, not pre-registered)
The encoding comparison so far confounds two things: whether a code uses spike
timing, and which features it encodes at all. Latency gates at
$\theta_\mathrm{lat}$ and delta at $\theta_\delta$, so the two encode different
feature sets. We separate them with a construction that removes the difference
entirely. Delta applied to a static vector fires exactly where
$x_i>\theta_\delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:res-delta}); setting
$\theta_\delta=\theta_\mathrm{lat}$ therefore makes it fire on precisely the
features latency encodes. The two arms then emit \emph{identical input spike
sets} and differ only in whether the spike carries timing information.

The construction verifies numerically: across all five confirmation protocols
the mean input spikes per sample of the two arms agree exactly, to a maximum
absolute difference of $\FNTimingMaxDiff$ (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). Any
difference in detection quality between them is therefore attributable to
temporal structure alone.

% table generated: paper/tables/tab_timing.tex
\input{tables/tab_timing}

The result is that timing has no general value. Its contribution is
$\FNTimingCtuA$~pp on CTU-13 fold~0 and $\FNTimingCic$~pp on CIC-IDS2017,
$\FNTimingNsl$~pp on NSL-KDD and $\FNTimingKdd$~pp on KDDCup99, and
$\FNTimingCtuB$~pp on CTU-13 fold~1: a spread of $\FNTimingSpread$
percentage points that changes sign. Temporal coding is worth more than half the
metric range on one protocol, nothing on two, and is a liability on another.
Timing-based coding is therefore not a property that helps or hurts in general;
its value is a property of the traffic distribution, and on the hardest
scenario-disjoint condition it is a liability.

This also settles the CTU-13 fold~1 inversion, which we previously reported
without a mechanism. Matching the gates in both directions leaves it intact:
moving latency to delta's gate gains only $\FNGateGainCtuB$~pp, and delta
retains $\FNDeltaKeepsCtuB$~pp of advantage at latency's gate. Since the gate
was the only alternative explanation and it is eliminated in both directions,
the inversion is attributable to the temporal component itself.

\paragraph{Distributional shift does not explain it either.}
% results/analysis/covariate_shift/
The most shifted condition in the study is CTU-13 fold~1 on every measure we
apply (median per-feature KS $\FNShiftKsCtuB$, train-versus-test
domain-classifier AUC $\FNShiftAucCtuB$, against $\FNShiftAucKddLo$--%
$\FNShiftAucKddHi$ for the KDD family), which substantiates its description as
the hardest fold distributionally rather than only by performance. Shift
magnitude does not, however, explain when timing is valuable: across the
\FNShiftNProto{} confirmation protocols the rank correlation between shift and
the timing premium is $\FNShiftRho$, and the two most-shifted protocols sit at
opposite extremes of it ($\FNTimingCtuA$~pp on fold~0, $\FNTimingCtuB$~pp on
fold~1). Having excluded both the amplitude gate earlier in this subsection and
distributional shift here, we report the conditions under which timing is a
liability without a mechanism for them. This diagnostic, its prediction and its
stopping rule were specified in
advance of the analysis and are reported despite a null outcome; it earns its
place by eliminating the most obvious alternative explanation rather than by
supporting our account.

It follows that an aggregate encoding rank averages over a factor that swings by
more than fifty percentage points between protocols. That is not a weakening of
the ranking result but the same thesis stated one level down: a ranking can be
stable while the mechanism producing it is not.

\subsection{Robustness and boundary conditions}
\label{sec:res-limits}

\paragraph{Exact duplication does not explain the encoding ordering.}
% results/analysis/dedup/
$\FNDupKdd\%$ of the official KDDCup99 test set and $\FNDupNsl\%$ of NSL-KDD's
occur verbatim in training. On the encoding axis, removing duplicated test
records changes absolute performance slightly but preserves both the leading
encoding and the complete ordering on all \FNDedupProtocols{} affected
protocols, and the median paired latency-versus-rate margin is essentially unchanged
(\FNDedupKddOff{} pp official against \FNDedupKddDed{} pp deduplicated on
KDDCup99). On the neuron axis the leading family is likewise unchanged
everywhere, although the full ordering does change on KDDCup99. The encoding
ordering is therefore not a consequence of memorised duplicate records.

\paragraph{One protocol-level ordering rests on too small a margin.}
Under the screening protocol, the KDDCup99 rate-versus-delta ordering rests on
a mean margin of $\FNKddRateDeltaMargin$ pp, with per-seed differences spanning
$\FNKddRateDeltaLo$ to $\FNKddRateDeltaHi$ pp and delta ahead in
$\FNKddDeltaWins$ of the five seed-blocks against rate's $\FNKddRateWins$. We
make no claim that either encoding is reliably ahead of the other there, and the
protocol-level rank for that pair should not be over-read.

\paragraph{CIC-IDS2017 lacks U2R test support.}
% results/analysis/AMENDMENT_cic_u2r_support.md
Under the order-disjoint CIC-IDS2017 protocol no U2R samples fall in the
held-out interval, because the mapped U2R events occur earlier in the capture
sequence. We retain the pre-registered five-class macro-F1, under which the
unsupported class contributes zero, for all ranking and statistical analysis,
and additionally report macro-F1 over the four classes represented in test as a
descriptive quantity that affects no ranking or test. For
\texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} these are \FNCicFixedLat{} (fixed five-class) and
\FNCicPresentLat{} (test-present classes). Because the absent class contributes
the same zero to every model, the two differ by a constant factor and induce
identical within-protocol orderings. The fixed five-class value is capped at
\FNCicCeiling{} on this protocol and must not be read as evidence that
CIC-IDS2017 is intrinsically harder than the other benchmarks.

\paragraph{Calibrated operating points do not always transfer.}
% results/analysis/security/fixed_far.csv
Thresholds are selected on validation to hold false alarms at $1\%$, frozen, and
then applied to test. Calibration itself is sound: realised validation FAR
lands on target in every case. Transfer to test varies sharply across all five
protocols. CIC-IDS2017 holds ($\FNFarValCic\%$ validation to $\FNFarTestCic\%$
test, DR $\FNFarDrCic\%$); KDDCup99 drifts mildly ($\FNFarValKdd\%$ to
$\FNFarTestKdd\%$, DR $\FNFarDrKdd\%$); NSL-KDD drifts substantially
($\FNFarValNsl\%$ to $\FNFarTestNsl\%$, DR $\FNFarDrNsl\%$); and the two CTU-13
folds fail in the same direction at very different magnitudes
($\FNFarValCtuA\%$ to $\FNFarTestCtuA\%$ on fold~0, $\FNFarValCtuB\%$ to
$\FNFarTestCtuB\%$ on fold~1) while both hold detection at $\FNFarDrCtuA\%$ and
$\FNFarDrCtuB\%$. Holding recall while the false-alarm rate moves by an order of
magnitude is the sharpest illustration here that the failure is one of threshold
transfer. Note that the FAR in Table~\ref{tab:strict-results} is the argmax
decision rule rather than this calibrated threshold; on CTU-13 fold~1 the two
read $\FNCtuBFarLat$ and $\FNFarTestCtuB\%$. This diagnoses
threshold-transfer instability under distribution shift rather than poor
discrimination: a detector may rank samples well while a threshold calibrated on
another distribution ceases to mean what it was set to mean.

\paragraph{Unseen attack types remain hard for every configuration.}
% results/analysis/known_unseen/known_unseen.csv
$\FNUnseenFrac\%$ of KDDTest+ attack rows belong to types absent from training.
Detection falls from $\FNKnownLo$--$\FNKnownHi$ on known types to
$\FNUnseenLo$--$\FNUnseenHi$ on unseen types, and this holds for all
\FNUnseenConfigs{} configurations of the design space rather than for a
selected subset. The finding concerns the task rather than the encoding ranking:
no evaluated encoding or neuron family transfers well to previously unseen
attack types, and latency's advantage is concentrated on types represented
during training. We do not emphasise the small spread among uniformly weak
unseen-type results.

\subsection{Summary}

\textbf{First}, \texttt{LeakyParallel} with latency encoding is the top-ranked
configuration in the complete \FNScrVariants{}-configuration \texttt{snnTorch}
screening study, at the head of a leading group that a Nemenyi test cannot
separate from it. Under strict targeted confirmation latency remains the
highest-ranked encoding (mean rank \FNEncRank, \FNEncWins{} of \FNEncBlocks{}
blocks) and \texttt{LeakyParallel} the highest-ranked neuron family
(\FNNeuRank{} of \FNNeuLevels), though both preferences are heterogeneous across
datasets and scenario folds.

\textbf{Second}, the ordering is not explained by exact train--test duplication:
removing duplicated records leaves the encoding-axis leader and complete
ordering intact on every affected protocol and preserves the latency--rate
margin.

\textbf{Third}, latency's advantage is not that it universally reaches decisions
earlier, it resolves earlier on two of five strict protocols and later on
three. What does generalise is compute demand: rate requires
$\FNSopRatioMin\times$ to $\FNSopRatioMax\times$ more synaptic operations on
every strict protocol. Predictive ranking, temporal behaviour and event
efficiency are three separate properties, and this study separates them.

It is worth stating plainly what the recommended configuration achieves in
absolute terms on the hardest of these protocols. On strict NSL-KDD it detects
$\FNNslDr$ of attacks at a $\FNNslFar\%$ false-alarm rate, and its macro-F1 of
$\FNNslMacro$ is held down almost entirely by the two rarest categories: R2L
recall $\FNNslRtlRec$ and U2R recall $\FNNslUtrRec$. Per-class figures for every
confirmation protocol are given in the appendix.

The scope of these claims is bounded by the same evidence. They imply nothing
about open-set robustness, since every configuration collapses on unseen attack
types, and nothing about deployable false-alarm behaviour under distribution
shift, since a validation-calibrated operating point can fail severely.
