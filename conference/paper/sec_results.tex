\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Design-space screening: the top-ranked configuration}
\label{sec:res-rank}

% results/analysis/v1/{mean_rank.csv,friedman.json}  ->  paper/tables/tab_ranking_v1.tex
Across all $27$ configurations evaluated over $20$ paired (dataset $\times$ seed)
blocks, \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} attains the lowest mean macro-F1 rank
($4.03$). A Friedman test rejects the null of equal performance
($\chi^2=263.9$, $p=3.2\times10^{-41}$), and the Nemenyi critical difference is
$9.28$.

That critical difference is the reason we describe this configuration as
\emph{top-ranked} rather than as statistically best: $14$ of the $27$
configurations lie within one critical difference of the leader, so the ranking
identifies a leading group and \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} sits at the head
of it. In particular \texttt{Leaky/latency} is statistically indistinguishable
from it ($p=0.33$), and we treat the two as co-leaders on terminal quality.

% results/analysis/v1/focused_tests.json -> paper/tables/tab_focused_v1.tex
The pre-specified comparison against the closest competitor,
\texttt{LeakyParallel/rate}, favours latency in $16$ of $20$ blocks with a
median difference of $+0.70$ pp macro-F1 (Holm-adjusted $p=0.0084$). The
hierarchical bootstrap over datasets gives $+3.79$ pp $[+0.11, +10.19]$. The
interval is wide because the effect is strongly dataset-dependent: it is
$+13.3$ pp on CIC-IDS2017 and near zero on the other three. At the encoding
level, aggregating over all nine neurons, the block-level test favours latency
over rate (Holm $p=0.027$) while the dataset-level bootstrap interval
$[-0.18, +11.30]$ includes zero. We report both. Taken together they describe an
effect that is consistent in direction but variable in magnitude, which is a
weaker and more accurate claim than uniform superiority.

We note one point of interpretation, because it is easy to reach the opposite
conclusion from the wrong metric. On KDDCup99, rate marginally exceeds latency
in \emph{accuracy} ($0.9260$ vs $0.9257$); on macro-F1, the pre-registered
primary metric for these heavily imbalanced datasets, latency leads by
$0.379$ pp. Fixing the primary metric before the analysis is what makes that
resolution credible rather than convenient.

\subsection{Strict confirmation along both design axes}
\label{sec:res-strict}

% results/analysis/v2_encoding/, results/analysis/v2_latency/
Under the leakage-resistant protocol we confirm the selection along each axis
separately. With the neuron fixed, latency remains the strongest encoding on
\NUM{n} of the evaluated protocols (Table~\NUM{tab}). With the encoding fixed,
\texttt{LeakyParallel} remains among the highest-ranked neuron families
(Table~\NUM{tab}). We report per-dataset means with across-seed standard
deviations throughout; no dispersion in this paper is pooled across datasets.

We stress what these two tables are not. Each holds one factor fixed, so
neither --- nor their combination --- constitutes a v2 ranking of the 27
configurations. That sweep was not run, and the complete 27-configuration
ranking remains the v1 screening result of
Section~\ref{sec:res-rank}. Sections~\ref{sec:res-rank} and this one answer
different questions and should not be merged into a single ranking claim.

% results/v1_submitted/ctu13_strict_vanilla.csv
The clearest effect of the strict protocol is on CTU-13, and it is large enough
that the original number should not stand. Under the submitted protocol ---
whole-scenario host aggregates with a random flow split ---
\texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} attains a perfect $100.00\pm0.00$ macro-F1.
Making the aggregation causal alone leaves $99.69\pm0.23$. Adding a
scenario-disjoint partition brings it to $\mathbf{92.01\pm9.72}$, with folds at
$99.9$, $99.9$, $91.2$ and $77.0$. We therefore report $92.01$ with its fold
range as the CTU-13 result, and retain the perfect score only as a labelled
protocol comparison. The fold spread is itself informative: it reflects genuine
differences in scenario difficulty that a uniform perfect score concealed
entirely, and it is the reason we never quote the mean alone.

This four-fold figure is the strict CTU-13 \emph{performance estimate}. It must
not be confused with the CTU-13 rows of the neuron-axis table, which come from
two preregistered folds and exist to rank neuron families against one another
under a strict protocol. Those rows are a comparative confirmation, not a
CTU-13 performance number, and their captions say so.

\subsection{Temporal evidence accumulation and spike efficiency}
\label{sec:res-readout}

This section establishes two findings that are easy to conflate and are not the
same claim: \emph{when} each encoding resolves its decision, and \emph{how much
event activity} it costs to do so. The first turns out to be protocol-dependent;
the second is consistent.

\paragraph{Evidence accumulation is protocol-dependent.}
% results/analysis/early_readout/summary.csv
On strict NSL-KDD evaluation, latency coding reaches $99\%$ of its terminal
macro-F1 at a median of $T_{99}=3$ timesteps against $20$ for rate, a
substantial opportunity for truncated inference. This behaviour does not hold
uniformly across datasets. Table~\ref{tab:readout} reports all four evaluated
protocols: latency also resolves earlier on CTU-13 fold~0, while rate reaches
its terminal operating point earlier on KDDCup99 and on CTU-13 fold~1. The
temporal benefit of latency coding therefore depends on the underlying traffic
distribution, and we report it as a dataset-specific result rather than as a
general property of the code.

The across-seed spread must be read with the medians. On NSL-KDD latency's
$T_{99}$ has an interquartile range of $2$--$9$ and a full range of $2$--$23$,
against $20$--$21$ and $17$--$23$ for rate; the distributions are largely
disjoint but one latency seed converges no faster than rate. We plot every seed
individually rather than a mean band, because the quantity is discrete and
skewed and a symmetric ribbon would misrepresent it.

We also note that a short $T_{99}$ measures rapid attainment of a
configuration's \emph{own} terminal performance and does not imply high-quality
early inference. Delta reaches $T_{99}=1$ on NSL-KDD while its terminal score
sits $6.5$ pp below latency's: that is rapid stabilisation at a weaker solution,
not an early-inference advantage.

\paragraph{Latency provides a consistently sparser event representation.}
% results/analysis/efficiency/summary.csv
The efficiency picture, unlike the timing picture, is uniform across protocols.
Three quantities must be distinguished, because only the first is structural.

\emph{Input activity} is a property of the code itself: latency emits at most one
spike per encoded feature, whereas rate draws Bernoulli samples at every one of
the $T$ timesteps. Measured, latency emits $12.5$--$22.2$ input spikes per
sample against rate's $238.6$--$309.1$: rate emits $13.9\times$ to $19.7\times$
more input spikes than latency across the four protocols.

\emph{Whole-network spike count} is not structurally guaranteed, since different
input timing produces different downstream firing, and the measured advantage is
correspondingly much smaller: rate emits only $1.9\times$ to $2.5\times$ more
total spikes than latency. The reason is that under latency coding the hidden
layer accounts for roughly $85\%$ of all spikes, and hidden firing differs
between the two encodings by only $1.15\times$ to $1.31\times$, so the large
input-layer saving is diluted in the total.

\emph{Synaptic operations} weight each spike by the fanout it drives. This is
where the input saving reappears, because latency's reduction in input activity
occurs \emph{before} the high-fanout input-to-hidden projection: input spikes
drive $128$ synapses each while hidden spikes drive only $5$. Input activity
accounts for $55$--$67\%$ of latency's operations but $94$--$96\%$ of rate's, and
the result is that rate requires $9.7\times$ to $13.0\times$ more synaptic
operations per sample than latency on every protocol evaluated. The reduction in
operations is therefore an order of magnitude larger than the reduction in raw
spike count --- \emph{where} spikes occur matters more than how many there are.
This is the one efficiency claim that generalises, and it is measured rather
than inferred from the input-sparsity argument alone. We report it as an
operation-count measure of compute demand; any conversion to energy remains a
proxy (Section~\ref{sec:eff}).

Delta is cheaper still, at $0.68$--$0.91$ of latency's SOPs, which is consistent
with its sparse transient representation. Latency is Pareto-optimal against both
alternatives on three of the four protocols; the exception is CTU-13 fold~1,
discussed next.

\paragraph{The principal exception.}
% results/analysis/efficiency/summary.csv, early_readout/summary.csv
On CTU-13 fold~1 --- the hardest scenario-disjoint fold, on which the strict
four-fold estimate is lowest --- delta encoding achieves the highest terminal
macro-F1 ($0.8825$) and latency trails by $11.2$ percentage points ($0.7702$),
with rate marginally ahead of latency ($0.7750$). This is the only protocol on
which latency is not the strongest encoding, and it is not a single-seed
reversal: the across-seed standard deviations are $0.0067$, $0.0050$ and
$0.0041$ respectively.

We report this without offering a mechanism. The present data establish
\emph{that} the encoding preference inverts under this scenario shift, not
\emph{why}, and a post-hoc explanation would not be supported by any experiment
in this study. The observation is nonetheless consequential for the reading of
the delta results generally: delta is weaker under the evaluated static-flow
representation in aggregate, but demonstrably capable of winning under a
sufficiently difficult protocol, so it should not be characterised as
categorically unsuited to this task.

\subsection{Where the advantage does not extend}
\label{sec:res-limits}

Three results bound the claim, and we report them because a configuration
recommendation is only useful alongside its operating envelope.

% results/analysis/security/fixed_far.csv
\textbf{Substantial operating-point drift under the official NSL-KDD
distribution shift.} A threshold calibrated to approximately $1\%$ false alarms
on the validation distribution produced about $7.7\%$ realised false alarms on
KDDTest+. We report four quantities together --- validation target FAR,
validation realised FAR, test realised FAR, and test detection rate --- so the
gap is attributable rather than ambiguous: validation realised FAR lands on
target ($1.013\%$ for a $1\%$ target), so the calibration itself is sound and
the drift is a property of the shift between KDDTrain+ and KDDTest+. The test
set is never used to select the threshold. The practical implication is that a
test-selected ROC operating point, as is commonly reported, would overstate
deployable performance.

% results/analysis/known_unseen/known_unseen.csv
\textbf{No encoding generalises to unseen attack types.} $29.2\%$ of KDDTest+
attack rows belong to $17$ types absent from training. Detection on those types
is poor for all three encodings --- $0.3481$, $0.3667$ and $0.3677$ for latency,
rate and delta --- against $0.77$--$0.80$ on known types. The headline finding is
therefore about the task, not about the encoding ranking: \emph{none} of the
evaluated encodings generalises well to previously unseen NSL-KDD attack types,
and latency's advantage is concentrated on types represented during training.
We deliberately do not emphasise the $1.9$-point spread among three uniformly
weak results. This experiment delimits the recommendation rather than supporting
it: \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency} is the strongest configuration for the
supervised detection task we evaluate, and that advantage does not extend to
attack types absent from training.

% results/analysis/security/duplicate_free.csv
\textbf{Benchmark duplication is large but does not account for the
conclusion.} $57.53\%$ of the official KDDCup99 test set occurs verbatim in
training. Restricting evaluation to the non-duplicated remainder costs $0.79$ pp
macro-F1 for \texttt{LeakyParallel/latency}, and --- the question that matters
--- leaves the ordering intact: the leading configuration is unchanged on both
KDDCup99 and NSL-KDD, the full ordering is unchanged, and the paired
latency-versus-rate margin is essentially identical on the deduplicated
remainder ($+0.98$ pp against $+1.05$ pp on KDDCup99). The overlap is severe
enough that absolute KDDCup99 numbers should not be read as evidence of
generalisation, but the encoding conclusion is not a claim about memorisation.

\subsection{Summary}

The study supports three claims, each stated at the strength the evidence
carries and no further.

\textbf{First}, \texttt{LeakyParallel} with latency encoding is the top-ranked
configuration across the complete $27$-configuration \texttt{snnTorch} screening
study, and remains the strongest encoding overall in the strict
\texttt{LeakyParallel} confirmation --- ranking first with a mean rank of
\NUM{rank} and the highest terminal macro-F1 in \NUM{wins} of \NUM{blocks}
protocol--seed blocks --- although its advantage is heterogeneous across
datasets and scenario folds. It
sits at the head of a leading group that a Nemenyi test cannot separate from it,
and it is not the strongest encoding on CTU-13 fold~1.

\textbf{Second}, the ranking is not explained by exact train--test duplication.
Removing duplicated test records leaves the encoding ordering unchanged ---
latency $>$ rate $>$ delta both before and after --- and preserves the
latency--rate margin, which moves from $+1.045$ to $+0.976$ pp on KDDCup99
despite $57.53\%$ of that test set being removed.

\textbf{Third}, latency coding's practical advantage is not that it
universally reaches decisions earlier. Its temporal advantage is pronounced on
some distributions --- on strict NSL-KDD evaluation it reaches near-terminal
macro-F1 at a median of $3$ timesteps against $20$ for rate --- while other
protocols favour rate for earlier convergence, and one difficult CTU-13 scenario
favours delta in terminal quality. What does generalise is event sparsity:
latency requires $9.7\times$ to $13.0\times$ fewer synaptic operations than rate
on every protocol evaluated. Predictive ranking, temporal behaviour and event
efficiency are three separate properties, and this study separates them rather
than treating them as one phenomenon.

The scope of that advantage must be stated with it. It does not imply open-set
robustness: all evaluated encodings show substantially reduced detection on
attack types absent from training ($0.35$--$0.37$ against $0.77$--$0.80$), and a
validation-calibrated false-alarm rate does not remain stable under the
KDDTrain+/KDDTest+ distribution shift. Latency's champion status applies to the
supervised configuration-selection problem this paper studies, not to open-set
detection.
