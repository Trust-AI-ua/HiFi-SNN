\subsection{When the student beats its teacher, and why this is not a paradox}
\label{sec:student_vs_teacher}

A distilled student is ordinarily expected not to exceed its teacher, and on
most of our datasets the GBDT teacher does define the quality ceiling: the
student tracks it from below, typically within one to two macro-F1 points
(Table~\ref{tab:encoder_ablation}). Two datasets appear to violate this
expectation. On CSE-CIC-IDS2018 the student reaches $98.0\%$ accuracy and
$75.9$ macro-F1 against the teacher's $82.7\%$ and $70.4$; on UNSW-NB15 the
student reaches $74.5\%$ accuracy against the teacher's $67.9\%$. The
explanation is that the teacher and the student do not optimise the same
operating point, so raw accuracy is not the axis on which the ceiling
argument applies.

The teacher is deliberately cost-sensitive: it is trained with
inverse-frequency sample weights ($w_i \propto N / n_{y_i}$, Section~3),
which shifts its decision boundaries toward the rare attack classes and
knowingly sacrifices majority-class accuracy for minority recall. The
student inherits the teacher's knowledge through temperature-softened soft
labels and the ranking term, but its dominant loss component is unweighted
cross-entropy, complemented by the false-positive-aware benign-confidence
term. The student therefore re-anchors its decision thresholds at an
accuracy-leaning operating point while retaining the teacher's class
ordering. The two models sit at different points on the same
accuracy-versus-balance frontier, and comparing them on a single axis can
favour either one.

UNSW-NB15 is the textbook case: the student leads on accuracy by $6.6$
points, but the teacher leads on macro-F1 by $8.9$ points ($50.6$ versus
$41.7$). Neither model dominates the other; the apparent contradiction is an
operating-point swap, and at the balanced operating point the ceiling holds
exactly as expected. CSE-CIC-IDS2018 is the genuinely interesting case,
because the student wins on both axes ($+15.3$ accuracy, $+5.5$ macro-F1).
Here the class imbalance is extreme enough that full inverse-frequency
weighting overshoots: the teacher over-predicts the smallest classes, its
precision on them collapses, and even its macro-F1 falls below the
student's. The student escapes this failure mode because distillation acts
as label smoothing over the teacher's overconfident posterior, a
regularisation effect known from born-again networks \cite{furlanello2018},
while the hard-label cross-entropy term re-anchors the majority classes.

As a control, we refit both teachers with the cost weighting disabled
(uniform sample weights, all else identical; seed 42). The accuracy-tuned
teacher recovers $98.5\%$ accuracy on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (from $82.6\%$) and
$77.1\%$ on UNSW-NB15 (from $67.9\%$), restoring the expected
teacher-above-student ordering on the accuracy axis in both cases. The
student's accuracy advantage is thus a property of the teacher's
cost-sensitive operating point, not of the tree ensemble's capacity. One
residual effect survives the control and supports the regularisation
account: on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 the student's macro-F1 ($75.9$) exceeds that of
the teacher at either setting ($70.4$ cost-sensitive, $73.1$
accuracy-tuned), a small but genuine born-again gain on the split where the
teacher's posterior is most miscalibrated.

Two practical conclusions follow. First, the GBDT row in
Table~\ref{tab:encoder_ablation} is a reference, not an accuracy ceiling,
and we label it accordingly. Second, the pairing is complementary by
design: the cost-sensitive teacher contributes minority-aware thresholds
and soft labels, while the student's loss restores majority-class accuracy,
which is why the distilled student can present a better
accuracy-versus-balance compromise than either objective alone.
